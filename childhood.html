JOURNAL OF DISQUIET

MEMORIES OF CHILDHOOD

Memory, Regret

From Games to the Ledger

I loved The Great Game of Bats, the flickering lights of the glow-boxes, and the thinking machines since I was five years old. I never used to miss a single match on the screen. I watched every ball of the long game, collected trivia, and traded cards with my few allies. I had many friends back then. I also loved the glow-boxes since the age of ten or even earlier. I saw the first game box back in the year of '95 at a kinsman's place and enjoyed watching others play. Sometimes, I got to play too. These were the times when I cared very little for the Ledger. I was an average kid when it came to the books. I was bad at The Logic of Numbers too. I remember getting a low mark in one of the tests when I was in the fourth level. I didn't care to show the report to The Patriarch. I had to forge his mark on the paper and give it back to the warden. The Patriarch would have caned me if he knew I had failed the test. Sometimes, I used to ask The Silent Guardian to sign, but she would refuse and send me back to him. I didn't worry much about it. All I wanted was to play. I spent evenings in the Great Game in my neighbourhood. Most of the holidays involved board games like The Checkered War or the game of bats outside. I was so much interested in the game that I wanted The Patriarch to put me through training. He refused. I watched one of my allies who played well; he went to the training and later represented The State. I would spend a lot of time watching the Moving Drawings on the screen. I remember waking up as early as the fifth hour to watch one. I enjoyed the glow-boxes so much that I would invite my allies over or go to their hideouts to play. I spent hours trying to beat the machine. One day, The Patriarch was moved, and we had to migrate to a new city. A city with no friends. All I had left was the home and the academy, and nothing in between. It was during this time that The Patriarch started taking interest in my books. He wanted me to study.

A Missed Opportunity

It was early morning when I reached the academy. My associate, The Whispering Messenger, was early too and wanted to talk in private. We walked the long corridors of the academy. He said there was a girl who had a crush on me, and he would only reveal her name if I was interested in pursuing her. I was surprised. A girl having a crush on me? A studious boy scared of the wardens, only caring about the marks. A risk-averse wallflower like me wouldn't think someone would notice me. I was eager to know, but I was scared. I said I couldn't go after a girl, as it would distract me from the Ledger and affect my marks. I found that a relationship at fifteen was too risky. I said, "I have to study and do well." I was pursuing a goal of being the top name etched on the silver plaque in the hall. The Whispering Messenger was taken aback. He said this was a chance at love and I was rejecting it for the books. I begged him to let me be.

After a few months, he told me her name. She was The Girl in the Hallway. She was beautiful. I remember my other associates used to stare at her. I had shared my notes with her once. She had been informal with me, and the other girls had giggled. I hadn't paid attention. But I never expected her to like an average-looking nerd like me. I wish I had at least spoken to her. It was too late. I remember glancing at her during the final test. She was busy writing, anxious, as the time ran out. I felt sorry for her. Had I pursued her, maybe I would have been a friend. She left the academy after that year and vanished into the fog. The Digital Registry didn't exist then, or it was the age of the old networks. I didn't have a machine, let alone the web. Six years later, I found her on the registry. She had moved to another country across the sea. I sent her a message, but it was like shouting into a void. She thanked me, but the connection was dead. I realized there is no point in chasing ghosts of lost love. Her name was The Girl in the Hallway. She was the one who got away.

I am also reminded by what The High Achiever, who was better than me at the books, told me: "By being shy and keeping away from them, what are you achieving? Aren't they just human? You don't know what you're missing. When you're old, you'll regret this." I never topped the class. My name was never on the plaque.

We Decide and You Do

After the tenth level, I had to choose between The Laws of the Universe, The Ledger of Souls, or The Arts. I spoke with a kinsman in the machine industry. I liked the logic of taxes and profit. He suggested The Ledger of Souls and the path of the Great Accountant. I was convinced. But The Patriarch rejected the idea. He gave me the silent treatment, snubbed me. I felt ostracized, as if I had spoken a forbidden word. He asked a neighbor to convince me to study the Laws of the Universe. They told me my marks were too good for the Ledger. I didn't know much then. I liked the math of business, but the Laws of the Universe involved the math of the stars and the curves—calculus and trigonometry—things that made my head spin. Finally, I surrendered to the Laws, because of the stigma of the other paths. The Arts didn't even stand a chance.

Trouble with the Laws

In the twelfth level, the Laws of Physics were a mystery to me. The Formulas of Chemistry were a burden. I was all alone, no tutor to guide me. I had to memorize what I couldn't understand. I was so scared of failing that I tried to skip the tests, pretending to be sick. I was scared of the warden of the Physics hall. I pleaded with The Patriarch to let me drop out and return to the Ledger. A relative stepped in and told me not to lose a year. I was taken to The House of Shadows—a mental hospital—to seek a healer for my mind. I was seventeen, sitting in a room with a professional, unable to speak my truth. The healing didn't help. I even thought of the final exit at one point. The pressure was a heavy stone on my chest. I started to meditate, sitting in the silence for half an hour every day. It helped me focus. I passed the final test, but the anxiety stayed with me like a shadow.

The First Acquaintance

When I was fifteen, The Patriarch introduced me to the daughter of his associate. Her name was The Scholar of the Archives. She was a scholar too. She wore specs and had a serious air. We exchanged books and notes. She worked harder than anyone I knew. I remember her math books had hand-drawn smileys—little charms to ward off the curse of the numbers. We met to exchange notes before the tests. Once, she came to my room and explored my shelves. Later, I went to her room and saw sketches on the wall—charcoal ghosts. We gave the exams, and she scored slightly higher. I didn't care. I wanted to play. I convinced The Patriarch to buy me a hand-held game box. I lost myself in the 3D worlds and forgot The Scholar of the Archives. She called me later, asking about a coaching center. I didn't listen. I thought I could walk the path alone. We stopped talking. Two years later, I saw her on a bicycle. She wished me luck, but her voice was cold. She was focused on the elite colleges; I was just a memory. Years later, The Patriarch told me she had a high-paying job in a private guild. I was working for the public bureau, feeling the shame of my choices. I tried to find her on the Digital Registry, but she had a thousand followers and didn't see me. She went on to get a PhD in a prestigious hall abroad. I envy her success. We had the same spark once, but our paths diverged. She was my first friend, and maybe more in my secret heart. But she never looked back.
